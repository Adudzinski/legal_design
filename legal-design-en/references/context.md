## 1. What legal design is

Legal design applies human-centered design to law, so that legal systems, services, and documents are usable by the people they affect. Key names: Margaret Hagan (Stanford Legal Design Lab; her open book *Law by Design* is a foundational source), Helena Haapio (proactive law, contract design), Stefania Passera (contract visualization, co-creator of the WorldCC Contract Design Pattern Library).

**Plain language and legal design are not the same thing.** Plain language is about words and sentences. Legal design also covers structure, layout, visual elements, and the reader's whole journey through a document. This skill covers only the language layer, deliberately — the language layer can be improved without adding or moving content, and the structural layer can't.

**ISO 24495-2:2025**, "Plain language — Part 2: Legal communication," published August 2025, is the international standard for this work, in any language. It organizes guidance around four principles carried over from Part 1: **Relevant** (the reader gets what they need), **Findable** (sensible headings, clear numbering and cross-references), **Understandable**, **Usable**.

## 2. Legal basis in the UK

**Consumer Rights Act 2015, s.68 (Requirement for transparency)** requires that a written term of a consumer contract, or a written consumer notice, be transparent — meaning expressed in plain and intelligible language, and legible. This is a statutory duty, not a stylistic preference: rewriting a set of terms into plain English is discharging an existing obligation, not creating new exposure.

**Consumer Rights Act 2015, s.69 (Contract terms that may have different meanings)** provides that where a term could be read in more than one way, the interpretation most favourable to the consumer prevails. This is the strongest practical argument for plain English in the UK: **ambiguity already works against whoever drafted the term.** Clarity reduces risk; it doesn't create it. This is the direct UK analog of the *contra proferentem* principle, now given statutory force for consumer contracts specifically.

**UK GDPR, Art. 12(1)** (retained from EU GDPR post-Brexit) requires that information given to data subjects be concise, transparent, intelligible, and in clear and plain language. Same obligation as its EU counterpart, applied to English-language notices.

**FCA Consumer Duty (Handbook PRIN 2A), the "consumer understanding" outcome** requires firms to support customers to make informed decisions, which the FCA has interpreted to include testing communications for comprehension, not just technical accuracy. The FCA has published good-practice and poor-practice examples specifically on this outcome.

**Industry guidance:** the Association of British Insurers, working with Fairer Finance, publishes a clear-communication guide recommending sentences under 29 words (30+ is "very difficult"), an Automated Readability Index (ARI) grade of 8 or below, headings phrased as the questions customers actually ask, and definitions placed at the point of use rather than front-loaded. It notes that the average UK insurance policy currently reads at a 16–18 grade level — far above what a general reader can comfortably process.

## 3. Legal basis in the US

**SEC Plain English Rule, 17 CFR 230.421**, applies to prospectuses. Subsection (b) requires clear organization, short explanatory sentences, bullet lists where possible, sparing use of a glossary (only defining a term where its meaning isn't clear from context), and avoiding legal and technical business jargon. Subsection (d) sets a heightened standard for cover pages, summaries, and risk-factor sections: active voice, short sentences, tabular or bulleted presentation of complex material, and no multiple negatives.

**New York General Obligations Law §5-702** requires that written agreements for residential leases, personal-property leases, and consumer transactions be "written in a clear and coherent manner using words with common and everyday meanings" and be "appropriately divided and captioned by its various sections." It sets no quantitative readability test — the standard is qualitative — but backs it with a real remedy: actual damages plus a $50 statutory penalty per violation (capped at $10,000 in a class action). A violation doesn't void the agreement or provide a defense to enforcement, and good-faith compliance attempts are exempt from penalty.

**State insurance-readability statutes** are the most concrete plain-language mandate in US law, because — unusually — they specify an actual numeric score. Nearly every state has adopted some version of the NAIC's readability model: most set a **Flesch Reading Ease floor of 40**; Connecticut, Florida, and New York require **45**; Maine, Massachusetts, North Carolina, Virginia, and Wisconsin (Medicare Supplement policies) require **50**. Texas requires a ninth-grade reading level or lower for individual accident-and-health policies; Rhode Island requires eighth grade or lower for health policies. Florida and New York also require a table of contents for policies over 3,000 words or three pages, minimum 10-point type, and clear section captions. A handful of states (among them Alabama, Illinois, and Louisiana) have no such requirement on record.

**Plain Writing Act of 2010** — a common point of confusion, worth stating plainly: **this applies only to federal executive agencies**, not to private companies or consumer contracts. It requires agencies to designate a plain-writing officer, train staff, publish a compliance plan, and take public feedback — but it bars judicial review of noncompliance and creates no private right of action. Don't cite it as binding on a private company's terms of service; cite the insurance-readability statutes or NY GOL §5-702 instead where they apply, or the FTC's general "clear and conspicuous" disclosure doctrine for consumer advertising and disclosures more broadly.

## 4. Readability targets

| Measure | Target | Where it applies |
|---|---|---|
| Flesch Reading Ease | 60–70 for general consumer text; ≥40–50 where a specific US state insurance statute sets a floor | US, and generally useful anywhere as a sentence/word-length proxy |
| Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level | roughly 8th–10th grade for consumer text | US, secondary metric |
| Automated Readability Index (ARI) | grade 8 or below | UK insurance-industry guidance (ABI/Fairer Finance) |
| Sentence length | under 25–29 words | both |

**Flesch Reading Ease interpretation bands:** 90–100 very easy (an average 11-year-old); 60–70 "plain English" (13–15-year-old reading level — the usual consumer-text target); 50–60 fairly difficult (10th–12th grade); 30–50 difficult (college level). For calibration: Reader's Digest sits around 65; Time magazine around 52; the Harvard Law Review in the low 30s. A typical unedited terms-of-service document is usually well below the consumer target.

Tools: Hemingway Editor, Readable.com, and Microsoft Word's built-in readability statistics (which report both Flesch Reading Ease and Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level directly).

**Caveat found in practitioner criticism** (see §6): these formulas measure sentence and word length, not comprehension. A short sentence with a buried nested condition can score well and still mislead a reader. Treat the score as a floor to clear, not a substitute for the Step 3 meaning audit.

## 5. Exemplar documents and organizations

- **Clarity International** (clarity-international.org) — the international association promoting plain legal language; publishes the *Clarity* journal and lists member organizations and case studies worldwide.
- **plainlanguage.gov** — the US federal government's plain-language style guide, built for the Plain Writing Act but widely used as a general reference well beyond government documents.
- **CFPB "Know Before You Owe" mortgage disclosure forms (TRID)** — the standard US exemplar of a two-layer pattern (a short, plain summary above the full legal disclosure) applied to a real consumer financial product.
- **ABI / Fairer Finance, "A guide to clear communication"** (2023) — the UK insurance-industry practitioner guide referenced throughout this skill.
- **Joseph Kimble, *Lifting the Fog of Legalese: Essays on Plain Language*** — a widely cited collection making the evidence-based case for plain legal writing, with before/after rewrites.
- **Bryan Garner, *Legal Writing in Plain English*** and **Richard Wydick, *Plain English for Lawyers*** — the two most commonly assigned plain-English drafting textbooks in US legal education.

## 6. Risk and criticism

**Does simplifying create legal risk?** It can, if content is lost or new ambiguity is introduced. But in the UK, CRA 2015 s.69 already resolves ambiguity against the drafter, so precise plain-language drafting usually *reduces* exposure rather than creating it — the same logic that applies in Poland under k.c. art. 385 §2. In the US, the position is less uniform: common-law *contra proferentem* is widely applied by courts against drafters, but there's no single nationwide statute doing what CRA s.69 does; NY GOL §5-702's per-violation penalty is the sharpest direct incentive. The risk sits in careless simplification, not in simplification itself — which is exactly why the audit step exists.

**What to never simplify:** mandatory disclosure content required by GDPR/UK GDPR Art. 13/14, SEC-mandated risk-factor language, FCA-mandated risk warnings, US state-mandated insurance notices; statutory cross-references; defined terms carrying legal effect; rights, deadlines, and complaint procedures; the allocation of liability and intellectual property. Simplify how something is expressed, not what it says.

**Criticism of readability formulas.** Industry commentary (including from within the insurance sector itself) has questioned whether meeting a Flesch score actually produces a document people understand — the formulas reward short words and short sentences without checking whether the underlying idea is clear. Treat a passing score as necessary, not sufficient; it doesn't substitute for the adversarial meaning audit in Step 3.

**The two-layer pattern** — a plain summary above the full binding text — is the standard way to gain readability without weakening enforceability. The CFPB's mortgage disclosure forms and the EU/UK's layered privacy notices (following the Article 29 Working Party / EDPB transparency guidelines) are the leading examples. Because it adds content, it's out of scope for this skill and needs a separate, lawyer-reviewed decision — but it's worth mentioning to the user as the natural next step once the language layer is done.

**The honest limit.** Some terms have to stay technical. Handle that by defining them once, in plain words, and using them consistently afterward, rather than rephrasing at every occurrence. In a legal document, consistency is worth more than elegant variation.
